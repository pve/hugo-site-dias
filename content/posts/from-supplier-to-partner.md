Promise theory helps us understand and pin down the relationship between a customer and a vendor.
As a customer you benefit from these fixed promises and contracts, they bring certainty, economies of scale, and market pressure on pricing.
But they also lock you in.
If you like fixed suppliers, you will have to do all innovation yourself.

It is more interesting to innovate with business partners, for the same reason that supply chains are a good way to distribute and scale competence.
But it has challenges.

Promise theory helps us understand that innovation requires shifting boundaries between suppliers and customers.
And those are soft boundaries in the early days of an innovation.
As a result, the promises that go with the 'new thing' are fluid as well.
It goes a bit like this:

> Hey, I have this new gizmo thing/service, and I thought it might be useful to you. I am not exactly sure how it works, and what the risks are, but it looks promising.

Some innovations are a little more mundane than that.
When I was with EDS I once witnessed a conversation where the EDS side picked apart a customer support process from the client in order to figure out if a part of that could be done with more scale and quality than the client could.
Of course, the client at first had no idea what the exact process promise was that would be optimal for them.
That was what they were going to find out.

How can this go wrong?
Typical friction or outright conflict in such a developing relationship is related to one or more of the following.

- Not enough clarity about the actual promises, or their quality. -example- how good is an LLM?
- Differing risk tolerances on the quality of the promise delivery.
- A partnership often develops new customers. Who owns the new customer?
- A partnership often develops new intellectual property. Who owns that property?

As these are sources of conflict, it takes governance to resolve them.
This governance has to come from the senior leadership that saw the benefit of the partnership in the first place, 
but if they lack sufficient understanding of the details of the promises, this is going to be a hard call.
Trust me, I have seen it all.

The tell tale signs of governance failures are when the people approving the partnership can't express the promises and their value in a single sentence, or can't resolve the friction points on their level.

What I like: the promise-theory framing is exactly right — "fixed promises lock you in, so innovation means doing it yourself" is a sharp, non-obvious opening claim that respects my intelligence. The EDS anecdote lands well too: a client not even knowing what its own optimal process promise looks like is a real, specific, slightly uncomfortable truth I've lived through myself. That's the kind of detail that makes me trust the author knows the territory, not just the theory.

What's unclear or thin: the piece changes altitude without warning. It opens abstract (promise theory, boundaries), drops into one vivid concrete scene (EDS), then jumps back to a bulleted list of abstract failure modes. I want either more scenes like the EDS one, or an explicit signal that the bullets are the "pattern behind" that scene. As written, the bullets read like a generic risk-management checklist — "differing risk tolerances," "who owns the new customer" — these are true, but I've read them in a hundred consulting decks. The -example- placeholder on the first bullet is the tell: you know it needs one too.

Where I want more depth: the governance paragraph at the end is the one that matters most to my role, and it's the thinnest. "Trust me, I have seen it all" is a claim, not evidence — I want the story behind it. What does it actually look like when senior leadership approves a partnership but doesn't understand the promise details? A stalled decision? A contract that gets signed and then reinterpreted six months in? Give me one failure mode with teeth, the way you gave me the EDS scene for the opening.

Assumption I'd push back on: "innovation requires shifting boundaries between suppliers and customers" is stated as if it's self-evidently true. I'd want to know — shifting relative to what baseline? Some of the best supplier relationships I've run were fixed-boundary and still innovative, because the innovation happened inside the contract's slack, not by renegotiating who does what. Worth at least a sentence acknowledging that fixed boundaries aren't always the enemy of innovation — otherwise it reads as a rule rather than a tendency.

Two suggestions:
1. Pick one of the four bullets — probably "who owns the new customer," since it's the most concrete and least abstract — and turn it into a second short scene, the way EDS did for the opening. That would make the failure-mode list feel earned rather than asserted.
2. End with the governance point sharpened into an actual test a reader can apply: something like "if the people approving the partnership can't describe the new promise in one sentence, they're not ready to govern it." Right now it ends on an anecdote-shaped assertion ("I have seen it all") without giving me the tool to check my own situation against it.
